Working Papers

peer effectsLee, A.

Running with the Crowd: Peer Effects Using Evidence from Marathon Runners

Abstract

We exploit quasi-random variation in peer group assignment at the New York City Marathon — runners are assigned to waves based on a best-pace threshold, generating a regression discontinuity in peer composition at each cutoff — to estimate peer effects on individual performance and pacing strategy. Using finisher data from 2023–2025 (n=56,613), we find that assignment to a slower wave has no significant effect on overall finishing time, but meaningfully distorts within-race pacing: runners assigned to slower waves run 1% faster than their predicted pace in the first 5K but 1% slower in the second half. Effects are largest among runners without an affinity for round-number finish times, suggesting peer influence is strongest when runners lack independent pacing anchors. The findings suggest that grouping policies can shape not just total output, but the intertemporal allocation of effort within a performance period.

educationLee, A.*, Aurino, S., Wolf, S.

Food Insecurity and Children's Cognitive and Socioemotional Skills: Whose Report Matters?

Abstract

Household food insecurity is typically measured using caregiver reports, but children's own reports may capture experiences caregivers overlook. Using a novel validated measure of child-reported food insecurity alongside caregiver reports for 3,771 children (ages 5–17) across five regions of northern Ghana, we find caregivers were nearly twice as likely as children to report food insecurity. In value-added models, caregiver-reported food insecurity predicted lower literacy and numeracy — especially for boys and older adolescents — while child-reported food insecurity was not predictive of any outcome. Findings suggest caregiver reports better capture the constraints food insecurity places on children's learning in this context, with implications for how interventions are targeted.

higher edBonilla, S.*, Lee, A., Minaya, V.

A Profile of Community College Students' Campus Engagement and Well-Being

Abstract

Community colleges enroll a disproportionately high share of low-income, racially minoritized, first-generation, immigrant, and older students, but the sector has faced declining enrollment and rising competition over the past decade — challenges sharpened by the pandemic. This descriptive study characterizes a first-time, low-income community college population and examines the relationship between students' time use, engagement with campus resources, financial and mental well-being, and overall campus engagement, highlighting where policymakers and institutions might prioritize support.
